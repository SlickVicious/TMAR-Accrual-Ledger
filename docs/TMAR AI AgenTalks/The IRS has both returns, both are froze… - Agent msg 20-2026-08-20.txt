PART THREE — THE LEGAL THEORY AND ITS SPECIFIC DEFICIENCIES

The legal theory employed on both returns rests on three statutory citations: IRC Section 3401(a), which defines wages for withholding purposes, 31 USC Sections 5103, 5115, and 5118, which govern the legal tender status of United States currency and Federal Reserve notes, and 26 USC 3503, which addresses erroneous payments.

The theory as constructed by the preparer operates as follows. First, wages are defined under Section 3401(a) as remuneration for services performed by an employee for an employer. The preparer argues that because the payments received did not constitute wages within that definition — either because no employer-employee relationship existed in the statutory sense, or because the payments were made in Federal Reserve notes which are instruments of debt rather than taxable income — the amounts reported on the W-2s were erroneous and subject to correction via Form 4852. Second, the 31 USC citations are used to classify the Federal Reserve notes received as legal tender instruments rather than income, relying on a theory that currency printed by the Federal Reserve is not lawful money and therefore not taxable. Third, 26 USC 3503 is cited as authority for treating the withholding as an erroneous payment that can be recovered as a refund.

The specific deficiencies in this theory are as follows.

The Section 3401(a) argument fails on the facts. The W-2s for both Clinton and Syrina show that employer-employee relationships existed. Inter Technologies Corporation had Clinton on a retirement plan (box 13 checked), paid him $22,638 in wages, and withheld payroll taxes. Boys and Girls Clubs of Wayne County paid Syrina $51,750 and withheld FICA and Medicare taxes. These are standard W-2 employment relationships. The statutory definition of wages under Section 3401(a) encompasses remuneration paid by an employer to an employee for services. The mere assertion that no employer-employee relationship exists does not make it so when the documentary record shows otherwise. Every court that has considered this argument has rejected it, including most recently in cases decided under the Tax Court's expedited procedures for frivolous positions.

The 31 USC legal tender argument fails as a matter of established law. Sections 5103, 5115, and 5118 govern the denomination, printing, and legal tender status of United States currency. They do not address the taxability of income received in the form of that currency. The argument that Federal Reserve notes are not taxable because they are instruments of debt rather than lawful money has been rejected by every federal court that has addressed it, characterized uniformly as frivolous, and specifically listed in IRS Notice 2007-30 as a frivolous position. The Tax Court addressed this theory in detail in Schiff v. United States, T.C. Memo 2004-169, and its progeny.

The use of Form 4852 is improper on the 2025 return because the W-2s were issued and delivered. Form 4852 is authorized only when the employer or payer does not issue a Form W-2 or Form 1099-R, or issues an incorrect one. The W-2s from Boys and Girls Clubs and Inter Technologies are in the file. Filing a Form 4852 when an accurate W-2 exists and inserting $1 in place of actual wages while claiming the actual wages as withheld tax is a misuse of the form and is precisely the pattern IRS Notice 2007-30 identifies as a frivolous return strategy.

The 2022 return presents an additional complexity. The $13,000 Teachers and State Employees Retirement System distribution bears distribution code G, which indicates a direct rollover. A direct rollover is not taxable and generates no income. The preparer's use of Form 4852 to substitute a 1099-R for this distribution and assert it as a credit suggests the $13,000 rollover was included in the claimed payments, which would be incorrect. The $5,757 distribution with code 1 involved actual withholding of $1,151, which is a legitimate credit. However, the 2022 Form 4852 for that distribution lists it as a 1099-R substitution on the basis that the company "erroneously reported distributions as taxable," which is not a recognized basis for Form 4852 substitution of a 1099-R.
